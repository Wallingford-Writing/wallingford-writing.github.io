Juno has no lemons.
She has words.

So she opens a Word Stand at Wallingford Playfield. Neighbors drop requests in the box: a sign for a lost cat, a thank-you for the crossing guard, a how-to for a kid who has never flown a kite. Juno writes each one, fixes it up, and takes it to its reader. Then the reader does exactly what it says, and everyone finds out whether the writing worked.

Draft

One sentence at a time.

A request card says who needs what. Then the page fills up, a line at a time, and every line is built a different way.

  • Tap word tiles into order to build a sentence that sounds right.
  • Finish a sentence with the words you like best. All three are good.
  • Some blanks are all hers: type your own words, or borrow some.
  • Spelling words are heard, not shown, and built from letter tiles.

Fix up

Her own capitals. Her own end marks.

The draft is all small letters with no end marks, on purpose. Fixing it up is her job: tap a word to give it a capital, and pick how each sentence ends.

  • The first word, names, and the word I.
  • Telling, asking or shouting? A period, a question mark or an exclamation mark.
  • Nib checks, and gives the rule for the first line that still needs something. Nothing is ever marked wrong in red.

Reader test

"She is a cat." Three cats show up.

Every piece has one writer's choice, and whatever she picks gets written and really read. The reader does exactly what the page says.

  • A sign with no details finds the wrong cats. A how-to with the steps out of order drops a kite on a shoe. An invitation with no day gets guests on Friday and Sunday.
  • Then she changes that one sentence and tries again. The rest of the page stays.
  • "That is how writers learn" is the only thing anyone says about it.

Publish

Sign it. Pin it up.

When a piece has done its job, she signs it with her finger, picks a paper colour and pins it to the Corner Board.

  • Each piece is laid out for what it is: a sign is big and bold, a note has a greeting, a postcard has a stamp.
  • "Read it to me" reads it aloud with each word lighting up, so she reads her own writing.

The Notebook

And pages that are only hers.

After the first piece, the Notebook opens: blank pages and the keyboard, with twelve ways to start if she wants one ("If I had a kite…").

  • Nib's look is optional. It notices three things only: a sentence that starts small, a sentence with no end mark, and a small i.
  • It never judges ideas or spelling, and never stops a page from being saved.
  • The Word Jar brings back spelling words from her pieces, the ones she missed first.

For grown-ups

Wallingford Writing was made by a parent in Seattle for their own young writer, as a sister game to Maple Leaf Math, Licton Springs Language and Cascade Cardboard Creator. Here's what you can expect.

✏️ The writing process

Draft, fix up, share, revise, publish. It's the same loop writers use, with a real reader standing in for the red pen.

🔤 Mechanics she applies herself

Capitals for first words, names and I. Periods, question marks and exclamation marks. She fixes her own draft rather than spotting mistakes in someone else's.

🐝 Spelling in context

Two to four spelling words per piece, heard and built from letter tiles, each one a word the piece needs. Missed words come back in the Word Jar.

🗣️ Every line is spoken

Everything a character says is read aloud by your device's own voice. You can turn the voice or the music off.

⏳ No pressure

No timers, no streaks, no scores to beat. A reader who is confused is funny, never cross.

🔒 Private by design

No ads, accounts, analytics or tracking, and the app never goes online. Everything she writes is saved only on the device. Privacy policy

🏙️ A real neighborhood

Wallingford Playfield, Gas Works Park and Kite Hill are real public places. Every business, school and person in the game is made up.

📄 Then try it for real

The best outcome is a kid asking for paper: a sign for her door, a note for a neighbor, a postcard to someone far away.
